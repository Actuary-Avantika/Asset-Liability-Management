Overview

A simulator that decides how a fictional insurer should invest, using utility theory rather than raw expected return — because the strategy with the highest average outcome is not always the one a risk-averse institution should choose.

Investment strategy guide

The three portfolios represent different risk appetites. Think of them as a decision ladder: protect capital → balance risk and growth → pursue growth.

LOWER RISK
Conservative
Meaning: Capital preservation and liability matching come first. The portfolio keeps 93% in fixed income, with only a small equity allocation.
MODERATE RISK
Balanced
Meaning: Seeks a middle ground between solvency resilience and long-term growth, with 80% in fixed income and a moderate equity allocation.
HIGHER RISK
Aggressive
Meaning: Accepts greater volatility in exchange for higher long-term growth potential, with 65% in fixed income and a larger equity allocation.

What the four scenarios mean

Scenarios represent different economic environments. The model changes selected assumptions to test whether an investment strategy remains financially secure when conditions are not ideal.

ScenarioMeaningWhat changes in the model
NormalBase-case environmentUses the model's original expected asset returns and liability assumptions. This is the benchmark case.
Market DownturnWeak investment marketsEquity returns are reduced by 8 percentage points and bond returns by 1 percentage point, testing the portfolio against weaker markets.
High InflationRising costs and larger future obligationsProjected liabilities are increased by 10%, testing whether assets remain sufficient when the cost of meeting obligations rises.
Combined StressAdverse market + liability pressureApplies both the market downturn and the higher-liability shock together. This is the most severe scenario in the model.
Why scenarios matter: A strategy may look attractive under Normal conditions but become less suitable when investment returns fall or liabilities increase. Scenario analysis therefore tests the resilience of the ALM decision, not just its expected return.

What this model does

A financial institution holds assets today and owes a stream of future liabilities. It must choose how to invest — across government securities, corporate bonds, equity, money market instruments and infrastructure/other investments — balancing return against the risk of not being able to pay what it owes.

This simulator projects each strategy's assets and liabilities forward, computes a funding ratio (assets ÷ liabilities), and ranks strategies using a utility function that penalises risk — so a highly volatile strategy can lose to a steadier one even with a higher expected return, depending on how risk-averse the institution is.

Key idea. Two strategies can share the same expected return, but the one with less volatility is preferred by a risk-averse decision-maker. Utility theory is what lets the model express that preference mathematically.
