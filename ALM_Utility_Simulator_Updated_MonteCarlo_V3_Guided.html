What this model does

A financial institution holds assets today and owes a stream of future liabilities. It must choose how to invest — across government securities, corporate bonds, equity, money market instruments and infrastructure/other investments — balancing return against the risk of not being able to pay what it owes.

This simulator projects each strategy's assets and liabilities forward, computes a funding ratio (assets ÷ liabilities), and ranks strategies using a utility function that penalises risk — so a highly volatile strategy can lose to a steadier one even with a higher expected return, depending on how risk-averse the institution is.

Key idea. Two strategies can share the same expected return, but the one with less volatility is preferred by a risk-averse decision-maker. Utility theory is what lets the model express that preference mathematically.

Asset & Utility Analysis

Projecting each strategy forward and ranking it by risk-adjusted utility, not raw return.

Why gamma needs to enter the maths, not just sit on the page

With one expected outcome per strategy, a risk-aversion parameter can't change anything on its own — there is nothing uncertain to be averse to yet. This model uses the standard mean-variance certainty equivalent approximation to fold volatility into the comparison:

CEreturn = μR − (γ/2) × σR2

A strategy's certainty-equivalent terminal wealth is then run through the utility function itself, so the displayed utility score reflects both return and risk, exactly once each.

How to read the simulation

Each simulation is one possible future. Returns are generated year by year using the asset volatility and correlation matrix. The model grows the portfolio, pays each year's liability, and records the lowest funding ratio reached along the path. A shortfall occurs if that minimum funding ratio falls below 100%.

Monte Carlo therefore adds an outcome distribution to the deterministic utility model. The deterministic model uses a certainty-equivalent approximation; the Monte Carlo engine estimates expected utility directly from simulated funding outcomes.

08

Methodology

Every formula used: what it means, why it's appropriate, and its limitations.

Present value of liabilities

PV(Lt) = Lt / (1 + r)t

Meaning. The value today of a payment Lt due in t years, given time value of money.

Why appropriate. Standard actuarial/financial discounting; lets cash flows at different times be compared on a common basis.

Variables. Lt = liability due in year t. r = discount rate.

Assumptions. A single flat discount rate applies to every year. Liability amounts are known with certainty in this version.

Limitations. Real liability cash flows are often uncertain in timing and amount; a full model would use a yield curve, not one flat rate.

Portfolio expected return and volatility

μR = ∑ wiri   |   σR2 = ∑i∑j wiwjσiσjρij

Meaning. Expected return is the weighted average of the assets' returns; variance depends on how the assets move together, not just their individual volatilities.

Why appropriate. Standard modern portfolio theory (Markowitz); ignoring correlation would overstate risk for a diversified portfolio.

Variables. wi = weight in asset i. ri, σi = expected return/volatility of asset i. ρij = correlation between assets i and j.

Assumptions. Returns are approximately normal; means, volatilities and correlations are constant over the horizon.

Limitations. Real returns have fatter tails than normal, and correlations often rise in crises — exactly when diversification is needed most.

Funding ratio

Funding Ratio = Assets / Liabilities

Interpretation. >100% means assets exceed liabilities; =100% exactly matched; <100% a funding shortfall.

Consistency. This model compares figures at the same point in time in both directions: Funding Ratio (today) = Initial Assets / PV(Liabilities); Funding Ratio (terminal) = Terminal Assets / FV(Liabilities), where FV(Liabilities) = PV(Liabilities) × (1+r)T — liabilities grown forward at the same rate used to value them.

Limitation. Liabilities are treated as a lump sum at the horizon rather than netted against assets year by year as they fall due. A full cash-flow run-off model would be more realistic.

Utility theory and the certainty equivalent

U(W) = (W1-γ − 1) / (1 − γ)   (ln(W) when γ = 1)

Meaning. A CRRA utility function — extra wealth adds less satisfaction the wealthier you already are; gamma controls how strongly that diminishing effect operates.

The deterministic-model problem. A single expected outcome has no risk to be averse to. To make gamma actually matter before Monte Carlo simulation exists, this model first computes a certainty-equivalent return, then evaluates U(W) on the certainty-equivalent funding ratio it implies (not on raw dollar wealth, which would round to an indistinguishable constant for any realistic gamma).

Limitations. This is an approximation, exact only for small, near-normal risks. A Monte Carlo extension would compute expected utility empirically instead.

Known limitations of this version

  1. Deterministic model: one expected path per strategy, not a full outcome distribution.
  2. Liabilities compared as a lump sum at the horizon, not netted year by year.
  3. Returns, volatilities and correlations assumed constant over the horizon.
  4. Stress scenarios are simple, transparent shocks rather than modelled macroeconomic events.
